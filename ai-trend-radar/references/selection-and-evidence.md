# Selection and evidence

## Category priority

Apply this default order while allowing an unusually consequential event in a lower category to rise:

| Priority | Category | Look for |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Models and core AI products | Material changes in capability, reasoning, multimodality, tool calling, access, weights, API behavior, context, price, speed, or cost. Explain what users can now do differently. |
| 2 | Agents and developer workflows | Longer useful execution, more complex tasks, new integrations, memory, harness effects, real organizational use, capability differences, and observed failure modes. Give this category substantial attention. |
| 3 | Research and benchmarks | Findings that change understanding, reveal a material weakness, or establish a new capability. Small leaderboard improvements normally rank lower. |
| 4 | Creative and multimodal workflows | Improvements in control, consistency, editing, speed, cost, video duration, and practical image/video/audio/music/3D/avatar production. A striking demo alone is insufficient. |
| 5 | Security, copyright, privacy, platform rules | Actual incidents, demonstrated vulnerabilities, experiments, and formal policy changes with identifiable consequences. Distinguish demonstrated impact from hypothetical risk. |
| 6 | Business and regulation | Structural acquisitions, strategic distribution, major partnerships, regulation, chips, compute, or cloud changes. Routine funding, earnings, executive moves, and promotional partnerships rarely justify a slot. |

## Six internal scores

Score each serious candidate from 1–10. These scores are editorial aids, not measured probabilities or an objective index. Keep them internal unless the user requests them.

| Dimension | Question |
|---|---|
| Freshness | Is there substantive new information in this run's window? |
| AI relevance | How directly does this affect AI technology, products, or development? |
| Industry impact | Could it materially affect users, builders, products, or the competitive landscape? |
| Discussion value | Does it pose a meaningful question, dispute, or testable claim? |
| Understandability | Can the intended audience understand its consequence? |
| First-hand evidence strength | How directly and reliably does inspected evidence support the claim? |

Use low, middle, and high scores consistently: 1–3 indicates weak support or relevance, 4–6 mixed or specialized value, and 7–10 a strong case. Do not let a summed score override insufficient evidence, an invalid time window, or duplicate identity. Community popularity is a secondary signal rather than a seventh ranking objective.

The final editorial question is: would skipping this cause the reader to miss an important change?

## Evidence and status

Separate these dimensions rather than collapsing them into one confidence label:

- **Availability:** announced, preview/waitlist, limited access, open for use, API available, or weights downloadable. Use only the state the evidence supports.
- **Claim provenance:** official assertion, vendor-reported measurement, independent test, paper result, firsthand developer observation, or attributed reporting.
- **Information status:** formally released; open for use; official preview; research result; developer test; security incident; substantive follow-up; media exclusive; or rumor/unconfirmed. Choose the most descriptive state and add an availability qualifier when useful.

Claims such as "80% better," "90% cheaper," "SOTA," and "10x faster" need the baseline and conditions where available. If only vendor results exist, explicitly say they are vendor-reported and keep confidence proportionate. An official announcement establishes what the vendor announced, not independent proof of superiority.

Reliable reporting about an unreleased model, leaked code, hidden API, or unconfirmed deal can qualify when the potential consequence is substantial. Attribute it and state that official confirmation is absent. Use conditional language for its consequences.

## Signal strength

| Rating | Meaning |
|---|---|
| 5/5 stars | One of today's most consequential changes, with a clear reason for broad attention. |
| 4/5 stars | A meaningful development worth tracking or trying. |
| 3/5 stars | Useful primarily to an identifiable specialist audience. |

Exclude weaker candidates from the final list. Stars express editorial importance; they do not certify factual confidence. A high-impact rumor can have strong potential importance and low confirmation, and the report must show both.

## Older events and repetition

Substantive new developments include formal release or access, material price changes, new independent tests, meaningful benchmark evidence, major bugs or security incidents, official responses, regulatory actions, and important developer evidence. Explain precisely what changed since the earlier report.

New coverage, reposts, continuing popularity, or another article quoting the same announcement do not qualify. Merge launch, API availability, and benchmark claims when they are parts of one release story.

For trends, require at least two independent underlying events and cite the relevant items. State a narrow observed pattern. If there is only one supporting event, present it as a watch item rather than an established trend. Say that no clear trend emerged when evidence is insufficient; do not fill a fixed quota.
